Curvilinear Coordinates and Rotating Frames

A working reference sheet: orthonormal bases and scale factors, line and volume elements, the four differential operators in polar, cylindrical and spherical coordinates, and the centrifugal and Coriolis forces written out component by component.

A clarification: this isn't a translation of any textbook, and it isn't the book itself. It's a reference sheet, my own condensed set of notes distilled while working through Nolting's course. Consider it a working reference I keep coming back to, not a substitute for the book.

01The basis and the scale factors

Fix two coordinates and let the third one run, and the point traces a curve. The derivative of the position vector with respect to that coordinate is the tangent vector, and its length tells you how many meters you move per unit of coordinate.

Tangent vectors to the coordinate curves

Start from a parametrization \(\vec r=\vec r(u_1,u_2,u_3)\) and differentiate with respect to each coordinate:

$$\vec b_i=\pd{\vec r}{u_i},\qquad h_i=\left|\vec b_i\right|,\qquad \hat{\vec e}_i=\frac{\vec b_i}{h_i}$$

That \(\vec b_i\) isn't unit length is exactly the useful part: a change \(du_i\) moves the point a distance \(h_i\,du_i\). If the coordinate is already a length, \(h_i=1\); if it's an angle, \(h_i\) converts radians into meters.

Cylindrical, worked step by step

$$\vec r(\rho,\varphi,z)=\big(\rho\cos\varphi,\;\rho\sin\varphi,\;z\big)$$
$$\Br{\rho}=\pd{\vec r}{\rho}=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi,0),\ h_\rho=1$$
$$\Ba{\varphi}=\pd{\vec r}{\varphi}=(-\rho\sin\varphi,\rho\cos\varphi,0),\ h_\varphi=\rho$$
$$\Bt{z}=\pd{\vec r}{z}=(0,0,1),\ h_z=1$$
The only factor different from 1 is \(h_\varphi=\rho\): turning by \(d\varphi\) sweeps an arc \(\rho\,d\varphi\), longer the farther you are from the axis. That same \(\rho\) resurfaces in the Jacobian and in the \(\rho\dot\varphi\) term of the velocity.
$$\Er{\rho}=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi,0),\qquad \Ea{\varphi}=(-\sin\varphi,\cos\varphi,0),\qquad \Et{z}=(0,0,1)$$

Spherical, worked step by step

$$\vec r(r,\theta,\varphi)=\big(r\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\;r\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\;r\cos\theta\big)$$
$$\Br{r}=(\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\cos\theta),\ h_r=1$$
$$\Bt{\theta}=r(\cos\theta\cos\varphi,\cos\theta\sin\varphi,-\sin\theta),\ h_\theta=r$$
$$\Ba{\varphi}=r\sin\theta(-\sin\varphi,\cos\varphi,0),\ h_\varphi=r\sin\theta$$

The last two already come factored as length times unit vector, so you can read off \(h_i\) without taking a square root. Moving \(\theta\) traces a meridian of radius \(r\); moving \(\varphi\) traces a parallel of radius \(r\sin\theta\), which shrinks near the poles.

Checking orthogonality

The simplified formulas below all assume an orthogonal basis, which follows from the dot products of the tangent vectors: \(\vec b_i\cdot\vec b_j=h_i^{2}\,\delta_{ij}\). The metric \(g_{ij}=\vec b_i\cdot\vec b_j\) is diagonal with entries \(h_i^2\), so the line element collapses to a sum of squares:

$$ds^{2}=\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}\,du_i\,du_j=\sum_i h_i^{2}\,du_i^{2}$$

Why the Jacobian is \(h_1h_2h_3\)

The Jacobian matrix has the tangent vectors as its columns, \(\mathsf J=[\vec b_1\,|\,\vec b_2\,|\,\vec b_3]\). Its determinant is the signed volume of the parallelepiped they span; with an orthogonal basis, that's a rectangular box of edges \(h_1,h_2,h_3\):

$$\det\mathsf J=\vec b_1\cdot(\vec b_2\times\vec b_3)=h_1h_2h_3\;\hat{\vec e}_1\cdot(\hat{\vec e}_2\times\hat{\vec e}_3)=h_1h_2h_3$$

The last step uses that the basis is orthonormal and right-handed, so \(\hat{\vec e}_2\times\hat{\vec e}_3=\hat{\vec e}_1\). Hence \({d}V=h_1h_2h_3\,du_1du_2du_3\) isn't a convention; it's just the volume of the box the three coordinate displacements build.

The four systems, at a glance

System\(u_1,u_2,u_3\)\(h_1,h_2,h_3\)\({d}V\)
cartesian\(x,y,z\)\(1,1,1\)\(dx\,dy\,dz\)
plane polar\(r,\varphi\)\(1,r\)\(r\,dr\,d\varphi\)
cylindrical\(\rho,\varphi,z\)\(1,\rho,1\)\(\rho\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,dz\)
spherical\(r,\theta,\varphi\)\(1,r,r\sin\theta\)\(r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi\)

02General formulas

With the \(h_i\) in hand, everything else is substitution. These hold for any orthogonal system.

$$\hat{\vec e}_i\cdot\hat{\vec e}_j=\delta_{ij},\qquad \hat{\vec e}_1\times\hat{\vec e}_2=\hat{\vec e}_3\ (\text{cyclic})$$
The basis depends on the point, \(\hat{\vec e}_i=\hat{\vec e}_i(u_1,u_2,u_3)\). Differentiating in time also differentiates the basis vectors; that's exactly where the centrifugal and Coriolis terms in the acceleration come from, with no new force involved.

Line, surface and volume elements

$$d\vec r=\sum_i h_i\,du_i\,\hat{\vec e}_i,\qquad ds^{2}=\sum_i h_i^{2}\,du_i^{2}$$
$$d\vec S_1=h_2h_3\,du_2\,du_3\,\hat{\vec e}_1\ (\text{cyclic}),\qquad {d}V=h_1h_2h_3\,du_1du_2du_3$$

Kinematics and kinetic energy

$$\vec v=\sum_i h_i\dot u_i\,\hat{\vec e}_i,\qquad v^{2}=\sum_i h_i^{2}\dot u_i^{2},\qquad T=\frac{m}{2}\sum_i h_i^{2}\dot u_i^{2}$$

Each component is \(v_i=h_i\dot u_i\): the rate of change of the coordinate, converted to meters per second.

Differential operators

Gradient

$$\nabla\psi=\sum_i \frac{1}{h_i}\pd{\psi}{u_i}\,\hat{\vec e}_i$$

Divergence

$$\nabla\cdot\vec a=\frac{1}{h_1h_2h_3}\left[\pd{}{u_1}(h_2h_3a_1)+\pd{}{u_2}(h_3h_1a_2)+\pd{}{u_3}(h_1h_2a_3)\right]$$

Curl

$$\nabla\times\vec a=\frac{1}{h_1h_2h_3}\begin{vmatrix}h_1\hat{\vec e}_1 & h_2\hat{\vec e}_2 & h_3\hat{\vec e}_3\\ \partial_{u_1} & \partial_{u_2} & \partial_{u_3}\\ h_1a_1 & h_2a_2 & h_3a_3\end{vmatrix}$$

Laplacian

$$\lap\psi=\frac{1}{h_1h_2h_3}\sum_i \pd{}{u_i}\!\left(\frac{h_1h_2h_3}{h_i^{2}}\pd{\psi}{u_i}\right)$$

Nolting writes the Laplacian as \(\Delta\); this page uses \(\nabla^2\) throughout, the same operator: \(\nabla^2=\Delta=\nabla\cdot\nabla\).

03Cartesian \((x,y,z)\)

The trivial case, and the one worth keeping in view: all three coordinates are already lengths, so \(h_x=h_y=h_z=1\) and every prefactor in the general formulas disappears. The basis vectors are constant, which is why their time derivatives vanish and no extra terms appear in the acceleration.

Gradient

$$\nabla\psi=\pd{\psi}{x}\,\hat{\vec e}_x+\pd{\psi}{y}\,\hat{\vec e}_y+\pd{\psi}{z}\,\hat{\vec e}_z$$

Divergence

$$\nabla\cdot\vec a=\pd{a_x}{x}+\pd{a_y}{y}+\pd{a_z}{z}$$

Curl, as a determinant

The usual mnemonic: unit vectors in the first row, the partial derivatives in the second, the components of the field in the third.

$$\nabla\times\vec a=\begin{vmatrix}\hat{\vec e}_x & \hat{\vec e}_y & \hat{\vec e}_z\\[2pt] \pd{}{x} & \pd{}{y} & \pd{}{z}\\[2pt] a_x & a_y & a_z\end{vmatrix}$$

Expanding along the first row gives the component attached to each unit vector:

$$\nabla\times\vec a=\left(\pd{a_z}{y}-\pd{a_y}{z}\right)\hat{\vec e}_x+\left(\pd{a_x}{z}-\pd{a_z}{x}\right)\hat{\vec e}_y+\left(\pd{a_y}{x}-\pd{a_x}{y}\right)\hat{\vec e}_z$$
Watch the middle sign. The cofactor of \(\hat{\vec e}_y\) carries a minus, which is what turns the naive \(\partial_z a_x-\partial_x a_z\) reading into the correct order. A quick check: the indices always run in the cyclic order \(x\to y\to z\to x\), so the \(\hat{\vec e}_x\) component involves \(y\) and \(z\) only, never \(x\).

Laplacian

$$\lap\psi=\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial y^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial z^{2}}$$

This is also the only system where \(\lap\vec a\) can be taken component by component, since the basis vectors carry no dependence on position.

04Plane polar \((r,\varphi)\)

The 2D case. The plane angle is the same azimuthal \(\varphi\) used in cylindrical and spherical coordinates, so formulas carry over without relabeling. \(\theta\) stays reserved for the polar angle measured from the \(z\)-axis.

$$x=r\cos\varphi,\ y=r\sin\varphi,\qquad \Er{r}=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi),\qquad \Ea{\varphi}=(-\sin\varphi,\cos\varphi)$$

Time derivatives of the basis: the step everyone forgets

$$\dot{\hat{\vec e}}_r=\dot\varphi\,\Ea{\varphi},\qquad \dot{\hat{\vec e}}_\varphi=-\dot\varphi\,\Er{r}$$
$$\vec v=\dot r\,\Er{r}+r\dot\varphi\,\Ea{\varphi}$$
$$\vec a=\underbrace{(\ddot r-r\dot\varphi^{2})}_{\text{radial, incl. centrifugal}}\Er{r}+\underbrace{(r\ddot\varphi+2\dot r\dot\varphi)}_{\text{angular, incl. Coriolis}}\Ea{\varphi}$$
$$r\ddot\varphi+2\dot r\dot\varphi=\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dt}(r^{2}\dot\varphi)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad L_z=mr^{2}\dot\varphi$$
If \(F_\varphi=0\) (a central force), the azimuthal equation gives \(\frac{d}{dt}(mr^{2}\dot\varphi)=0\): angular momentum is conserved, Kepler's second law in disguise.

Operators

$$\nabla\psi=\pd{\psi}{r}\Er{r}+\frac{1}{r}\pd{\psi}{\varphi}\Ea{\varphi}\qquad \nabla\cdot\vec a=\frac{1}{r}\pd{}{r}(r a_r)+\frac{1}{r}\pd{a_\varphi}{\varphi}$$
$$\lap\psi=\frac{1}{r}\pd{}{r}\!\left(r\pd{\psi}{r}\right)+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial\varphi^{2}}$$

05Cylindrical \((\rho,\varphi,z)\)

Plane polar plus an untouched \(z\)-axis. Since \(h_z=1\), \(z\) adds no extra factors. Note that \(\rho\) is measured from the axis, not from the origin.

$$x=\rho\cos\varphi,\ y=\rho\sin\varphi,\ z=z$$
$$\Er{\rho}=\cos\varphi\,\hat{\vec e}_x+\sin\varphi\,\hat{\vec e}_y,\qquad \Ea{\varphi}=-\sin\varphi\,\hat{\vec e}_x+\cos\varphi\,\hat{\vec e}_y,\qquad \Et{z}=\hat{\vec e}_z$$
$$\dot{\hat{\vec e}}_\rho=\dot\varphi\,\Ea{\varphi},\qquad \dot{\hat{\vec e}}_\varphi=-\dot\varphi\,\Er{\rho},\qquad \dot{\hat{\vec e}}_z=0$$
$$\vec v=\dot\rho\,\Er{\rho}+\rho\dot\varphi\,\Ea{\varphi}+\dot z\,\Et{z}$$
$$\vec a=(\ddot\rho-\rho\dot\varphi^{2})\Er{\rho}+(\rho\ddot\varphi+2\dot\rho\dot\varphi)\Ea{\varphi}+\ddot z\,\Et{z}$$
$$T=\frac{m}{2}(\dot\rho^{2}+\rho^{2}\dot\varphi^{2}+\dot z^{2})$$

Operators

$$\nabla\psi=\pd{\psi}{\rho}\Er{\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho}\pd{\psi}{\varphi}\Ea{\varphi}+\pd{\psi}{z}\Et{z}$$
$$\nabla\cdot\vec a=\frac{1}{\rho}\pd{}{\rho}(\rho a_\rho)+\frac{1}{\rho}\pd{a_\varphi}{\varphi}+\pd{a_z}{z}$$
$$\nabla\times\vec a=\left[\frac{1}{\rho}\pd{a_z}{\varphi}-\pd{a_\varphi}{z}\right]\Er{\rho}+\left[\pd{a_\rho}{z}-\pd{a_z}{\rho}\right]\Ea{\varphi}+\frac{1}{\rho}\left[\pd{}{\rho}(\rho a_\varphi)-\pd{a_\rho}{\varphi}\right]\Et{z}$$
$$\lap\psi=\frac{1}{\rho}\pd{}{\rho}\!\left(\rho\pd{\psi}{\rho}\right)+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial\varphi^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial z^{2}}$$

06Spherical \((r,\theta,\varphi)\)

Nolting's convention: \(\theta\) is the polar angle from the \(z\)-axis (\(0\le\theta\le\pi\)) and \(\varphi\) the azimuthal angle in the \(xy\)-plane (\(0\le\varphi<2\pi\)).

$$x=r\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\ y=r\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\ z=r\cos\theta$$
$$\Er{r}=(\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\cos\theta)$$
$$\Et{\theta}=(\cos\theta\cos\varphi,\cos\theta\sin\varphi,-\sin\theta)$$
$$\Ea{\varphi}=(-\sin\varphi,\cos\varphi,0)$$
$$\hat{\vec e}_z=\cos\theta\,\Er{r}-\sin\theta\,\Et{\theta}$$
$$\dot{\hat{\vec e}}_r=\dot\theta\,\Et{\theta}+\dot\varphi\sin\theta\,\Ea{\varphi}\quad\quad \dot{\hat{\vec e}}_\theta=-\dot\theta\,\Er{r}+\dot\varphi\cos\theta\,\Ea{\varphi}\quad\quad \dot{\hat{\vec e}}_\varphi=-\dot\varphi(\sin\theta\,\Er{r}+\cos\theta\,\Et{\theta})$$
$$\vec v=\dot r\,\Er{r}+r\dot\theta\,\Et{\theta}+r\sin\theta\,\dot\varphi\,\Ea{\varphi}$$
$$a_r=\ddot r-r\dot\theta^{2}-r\sin^{2}\theta\,\dot\varphi^{2}$$ $$a_\theta=r\ddot\theta+2\dot r\dot\theta-r\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\varphi^{2}$$ $$a_\varphi=r\sin\theta\,\ddot\varphi+2\dot r\dot\varphi\sin\theta+2r\dot\theta\dot\varphi\cos\theta$$
$${d}V=r^{2}\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi,\qquad d\Omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi,\qquad \oint d\Omega=4\pi$$

Operators

$$\nabla\psi=\pd{\psi}{r}\Er{r}+\frac{1}{r}\pd{\psi}{\theta}\Et{\theta}+\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\pd{\psi}{\varphi}\Ea{\varphi}$$
$$\nabla\cdot\vec a=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\pd{}{r}(r^{2}a_r)+\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\pd{}{\theta}(\sin\theta\,a_\theta)+\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\pd{a_\varphi}{\varphi}$$
$$\lap\psi=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\pd{}{r}\!\left(r^{2}\pd{\psi}{r}\right)+\frac{1}{r^{2}\sin\theta}\pd{}{\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\pd{\psi}{\theta}\right)+\frac{1}{r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial\varphi^{2}}$$

Radial term, alternative form: \(\dfrac{1}{r^{2}}\pd{}{r}\!\left(r^{2}\pd{\psi}{r}\right)=\dfrac{1}{r}\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}(r\psi)\).

08Identities and integral theorems

These hold in any coordinate system: they're statements about the operators, not about components.

$$\nabla\times(\nabla\psi)=0,\qquad \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec a)=0$$
$$\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec a)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec a)-\lap\vec a$$
Careful: \(\nabla^{2}\vec a\) acting component by component only works in Cartesian coordinates. In curvilinear systems, use the identity above; the basis vectors get differentiated too.
$$\nabla(fg)=f\nabla g+g\nabla f$$ $$\nabla\cdot(f\vec a)=f(\nabla\cdot\vec a)+\vec a\cdot\nabla f$$ $$\nabla\times(f\vec a)=f(\nabla\times\vec a)+(\nabla f)\times\vec a$$
$$\nabla\cdot\vec r=3,\qquad \nabla\times\vec r=0,\qquad \nabla r=\Er{r}$$ $$\nabla\!\left(\frac1r\right)=-\frac{\Er{r}}{r^2},\qquad \lap\!\left(\frac1r\right)=-4\pi\delta^{(3)}(\vec r)$$

Gauss

$$\int_V \nabla\cdot\vec a\;{d}V=\oint_{\partial V}\vec a\cdot d\vec S$$

Stokes

$$\int_S (\nabla\times\vec a)\cdot d\vec S=\oint_{\partial S}\vec a\cdot d\vec r$$

09Accelerated reference frames

\(\Sigma\) inertial, \(\Sigma'\) with origin at \(\vec r_0(t)\), rotating with angular velocity \(\w\) relative to \(\Sigma\).

The master transport rule

$$\left(\frac{d\vec A}{dt}\right)_{\Sigma}=\left(\frac{d\vec A}{dt}\right)_{\Sigma'}+\w\times\vec A$$
Everything below is this rule applied twice to the position vector. No extra physics is hiding: the inertial forces are just the price of writing \(m\ddot{\vec r}=\vec F\) in a non-inertial frame.
$$m\ddot{\vec r}\,'=\vec F\;\underbrace{-m\ddot{\vec r}_0}_{\text{translation}}\;\underbrace{-m\dot{\w}\times\vec r\,'}_{\text{Euler}}\;\underbrace{-2m\,\w\times\dot{\vec r}\,'}_{\text{Coriolis}}\;\underbrace{-m\,\w\times(\w\times\vec r\,')}_{\text{centrifugal}}$$
Centrifugal
$$\vec F_{\text{cf}}=-m\,\w\times(\w\times\vec r\,')=m[\omega^{2}\vec r\,'-(\w\cdot\vec r\,')\w]$$
$$|\vec F_{\text{cf}}|=m\omega^{2}\rho_\perp$$
Coriolis
$$\vec F_{\text{Cor}}=-2m\,\w\times\vec v\,'$$
$$|\vec F_{\text{Cor}}|=2m\omega v'\sin\alpha,\qquad \vec F_{\text{Cor}}\cdot\vec v\,'=0$$

\(\rho_\perp\) is the distance to the rotation axis, not to the origin: the centrifugal force points outward, perpendicular to that axis. Coriolis is perpendicular to \(\vec v\,'\), so it does no work: it bends the path without changing kinetic energy, and it vanishes for a particle at rest in \(\Sigma'\).

$$V_{\text{cf}}=-\frac{m}{2}\omega^{2}\rho_\perp^{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \vec F_{\text{cf}}=-\nabla V_{\text{cf}}$$

Centrifugal does admit a potential, since it depends only on position. Coriolis doesn't: it depends on velocity.

10Centrifugal and Coriolis, component by component

With \(\w=\omega\,\hat{\vec e}_z\) constant and the origin of \(\Sigma'\) on the rotation axis.

Cylindrical, the natural case

$$\w\times(\w\times\vec r\,')=-\omega^{2}\rho\,\Er{\rho}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \vec F_{\text{cf}}=m\omega^{2}\rho\,\Er{\rho}$$
$$\vec F_{\text{Cor}}=2m\omega\rho\dot\varphi\,\Er{\rho}-2m\omega\dot\rho\,\Ea{\varphi}$$

Notice \(\dot z\) never appears: motion parallel to the rotation axis feels no Coriolis force.

$$m(\ddot\rho-\rho\dot\varphi^{2})=F_\rho+m\omega^{2}\rho+2m\omega\rho\dot\varphi\qquad m(\rho\ddot\varphi+2\dot\rho\dot\varphi)=F_\varphi-2m\omega\dot\rho\qquad m\ddot z=F_z$$
Defining \(\Omega=\dot\varphi+\omega\) (the angular velocity seen from \(\Sigma\)), the radial equation collapses to \(m(\ddot\rho-\rho\Omega^{2})=F_\rho\), the familiar equation, rewritten. A good sign check.

Spherical

$$\w=\omega\,\hat{\vec e}_z=\omega(\cos\theta\,\Er{r}-\sin\theta\,\Et{\theta})$$
$$\vec F_{\text{cf}}=m\omega^{2}r\sin\theta\left[\sin\theta\,\Er{r}+\cos\theta\,\Et{\theta}\right]$$
$$\vec F_{\text{Cor}}=2m\omega r\sin^{2}\theta\,\dot\varphi\,\Er{r}+2m\omega r\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\varphi\,\Et{\theta}$$ $$\qquad-\,2m\omega(\dot r\sin\theta+r\dot\theta\cos\theta)\Ea{\varphi}$$

Sanity check: \(\rho_\perp=r\sin\theta\), and the centrifugal force has magnitude \(m\omega^{2}r\sin\theta\) pointing along \(\sin\theta\,\hat{\vec e}_r+\cos\theta\,\hat{\vec e}_\theta\): exactly \(\hat{\vec e}_\rho\), perpendicular to the \(z\)-axis, as it should be.
